# AKOÚŌ and changing attention

Use when the listener asks for AKOÚŌ, wants to experiment with attention, notices bodily responses, or finds that a passage changes when another voice comes into focus.

[AKOÚŌ](https://github.com/sonicfieldlabs/akouo) provides listening modes and a framework for keeping claims attached to their evidence. This guide selectively adapts its ideas to Music Impressions. The replay exercise and concerto example below come from our listening conversation; they are not an official AKOÚŌ protocol.

## Choose a focus and a useful check

Choose one focus that serves the live question. Add another when it could reveal what the first leaves out; an exhaustive tour of modes usually interrupts the encounter.

| Focus drawn from AKOÚŌ | Attend to | A useful check |
|---|---|---|
| Musical / aesthetic | Repetition, contour, rhythm, articulation, layering, and how material changes | Locate the detail supporting the impression in the score or recording. |
| Embodied / affective | Breath, posture, sway, bracing, suspension, propulsion, and changes in attention | Invite the listener's actual response before naming an emotion or proposing a dramatic meaning. |
| Signal inspection | A bounded question about timing, level, frequency, or recording conditions | Check whether the measurement addresses that question; whole-mix level cannot establish what one instrument does. |

Keep bodily reports particular to this listener and occasion. Playback, surroundings, expectation, and fatigue can matter; ask about them when they explain a concrete uncertainty. A wish to move, an effort to keep still, or no noticeable bodily response can each be worth staying with.

## Replay with changed attention

When the listener wants an experiment, choose a short passage they can replay unchanged. Offer one simple focus, such as following the solo line, attending to the accompaniment, or noticing bodily movement. Let them describe the result before suggesting a contrasting focus or a broader hearing of the whole texture. Leave the possible result open: the invitation should not tell them that the soloist is isolated or the orchestra oppressive.

Follow what became prominent, what receded, and whether the relationships changed. Carry a useful discovery back into the original passage or the surrounding music; repeating the exercise is optional.

In the originating concerto conversation, Ivan called this an “equaliser” of attention. Attending to the orchestra made the parts feel as though they shared a source and moved together. Attending to the cello made it feel separate, “floating above other voices.” Preserve these as reports of changing perception. A perceived common source does not establish a single physical source, and a voice becoming more prominent does not establish an increase in its recorded level.

Let this change a dramatic reading when appropriate. A scene of one person facing others already assumes separate participants; another hearing may loosen that assumption. Separation need not mean conflict, and shared movement need not mean agreement. Let the listener decide whether either description fits before extending the story. For developed personas, return to [Perspectives and personal reflection](perspectives.md).

## Use the method with the evidence available

AKOÚŌ's listening framework can guide conversation through the listener's reports, notation, and available measurements. OÍDA and audio models are optional ways to obtain additional observations. A model's output remains attributable to that model; a structured report does not verify its accuracy. For model tests and score matching, use [Recording and score investigation](recording-and-score.md).

Use ordinary conversation for a listening journey. This adaptation does not implement the full AKOÚŌ data contract. If the user requests a compatible structured export, consult and validate against the relevant upstream schemas rather than presenting this guide as schema compliance.
